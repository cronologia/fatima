Our Lady of Fátima — Chronology

Chronology of the reported apparitions at Fátima, Portugal, and of what came before and after them (1881–2017)

An open, source-referenced chronology of the reported apparitions of Our Lady of the Rosary at the Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal (1917): the anticlerical politics and the suppressed diocese that preceded them, the three seers, the Church's canonical judgment of 1930, the shrine, the pilgrimage and the seers' causes that followed, and the reported cures — recorded as accounts and as the Church acts that judged them, never as events.

How far the case went

Fátima's approval is DIOCESAN. The judgment that the visions are 'worthy of belief' was made by the bishop of Leiria in 1930; this chronology has located no act of the Holy See ruling on whether the 1917 apparitions were supernatural. Each rung below records one authority — what it did, when, and in which document — and the page adds them up into no overall verdict.

Step by step, with the documents

Parish and local clergy 1917–1919

—Investigated — no verdict issued

Fr Manuel Marques Ferreira, parish priest of Fátima (1914–1920), reporting to the Patriarchate of Lisbon: the Diocese of Leiria was restored in 1918 but had no resident bishop until May 1920.

The parish priest questioned Lúcia within about a fortnight of the first reported apparition and went on interrogating the children through 1917, keeping notes — but issued no judgment. Lúcia records him telling her 'Não me parece uma revelação do Céu' ('It does not seem to me a revelation from Heaven'), warning that it might be a deception and adding 'the future will tell us what to think'. He kept away from the Cova da Iria on the 13th of each month; accused in the press of complicity in the civil administrator's detention of the children on 13 August 1917, he published denials in which he called his 'absence and apparent indifference' deliberate, so that unbelievers could not attribute the crowd's faith to the parish priest. Ordered by the Patriarchate of Lisbon to gather depositions and report, he produced the parish report that became the main documentary basis of the later diocesan process, seeking in it to appear 'indiferente enquanto não houvesse provas evidentes ou a Igreja falasse' ('indifferent as long as there were no evident proofs or the Church spoke'). No favourable act exists at this level, because none was issued: the case was passed upward instead.

Diocese of Leiria — canonical process and judgment 3 May 1922 – 13 October 1930

✓Investigated — concluded in favour

José Alves Correia da Silva, bishop of the restored Diocese of Leiria, and the commission of inquiry he named — which included Canon Manuel Nunes Formigão, who had begun interrogating the seers on 27 September 1917.

By a provision of 3 May 1922 the bishop ordered the canonical process and named the commission; it reported to him in 1930. In the pastoral letter 'A Divina Providência' of 13 October 1930 he declared it right 'to declare worthy of belief' (dignas de crédito) the visions of the children at the Cova da Iria on the 13th of May to October 1917, and 'officially to permit the cult of Our Lady of Fátima' (Documentação Crítica de Fátima, doc. 133). This is the act Fátima's approval rests on. In Catholic teaching it judges a private revelation credible and permits devotion; it is not a dogmatic definition and obliges no one to believe.

The Holy See no Roman ruling located

·No record found that this step took place

The Roman congregations and the popes.

Rome has acted around Fátima many times, but none of the acts located here rules on whether the 1917 apparitions were supernatural. The nearest candidate is the letter 'Celeberrima sollemnia' of 28 April 1946, by which Pius XII sent Cardinal Benedetto Aloisi Masella as his legate to crown the image at the shrine (Acta Apostolicae Sedis 38, p. 376): an honour paid to an image, deciding nothing about the visions. The beatification (13 May 2000) and canonization (13 May 2017) of Francisco and Jacinta Marto are judgments about two children's holiness, not about what they reported seeing. 'The Message of Fatima' (26 June 2000) is what its title says — the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith publishing the third part of the 'Secret' with a theological commentary; it cites no decree of authenticity, and the commentary itself insists that even an approved private revelation obliges no one to believe.

Searched for a Roman act ruling on the apparitions' supernatural character, and found none. Checked: the complete document index of the Congregation (now Dicastery) for the Doctrine of the Faith on vatican.va, which lists no Fátima document other than the 26 June 2000 publication; that publication itself, which cites no decree of authenticity; and the Acta Apostolicae Sedis volumes from vatican.va, where 'Fatim*' returns nothing at all in vol. 22 (1930) or vol. 23 (1931) — the years around the diocesan ruling — and nothing in the 1940 index volume, while the control terms 'decretum' and 'Lusitan*' return dozens to hundreds of hits in those same files, so the silence is the source's and not the search's. In vol. 38 (1946) the Fátima entries are the coronation letter and Pius XII's radio message for the coronation, not a ruling. Papal pilgrimages to the shrine, consecrations of the world to the Immaculate Heart of Mary, indulgence and liturgical concessions, the two causes of the seers, and the 2000 publication with its commentary were each considered and set aside for the same reason: they are acts of devotion, of liturgy, or about persons. This is a record of what was and was not found — not a claim that Rome ruled against Fátima. Fátima's approval does not depend on Rome having ruled: it rests on the 1930 diocesan act above, which surprises many readers.

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). The apparitions are REPORTED events: this site records who reported what and when, and what the Church ruled and when, citing the ruling documents — it never asserts the supernatural claim as fact in its own voice. Reported miracles are handled the same way, and only two things attached to them are datable: that a cure was REPORTED — by whom, and when first attested — and any RECOGNITION ACT, a dated decision of the Church with a document behind it. No entry here says that a cure happened, aggregate claims are attributed to whoever makes them, and reported cures earn no rung on the approval ladder, which records judgments on the apparitions alone. The chronology also carries context before 1917 (the suppressed diocese, the First Republic, the 1911 Law of Separation) and the institutional history after it (the shrine, the pilgrimage, the seers' later lives and causes). Details attested only retrospectively (the 1916 Angel apparitions, the 'Secret') are flagged as such; dates not yet verified against a primary source carry a ? mark, and where sources disagree the disagreement is recorded rather than resolved. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A series of six Marian apparitions reported at the Cova da Iria, parish of Fátima, Portugal, between 13 May and 13 October 1917, by three shepherd children — Lúcia dos Santos and her cousins Francisco and Jacinta Marto. The figure they described later identified herself, in their account, as 'the Lady of the Rosary'.[1] [3]
The seers
Lúcia de Jesus dos Santos (b. 28 March 1907), Francisco Marto (b. 11 June 1908) and Jacinta Marto (b. 5 March 1910), all of Aljustrel, parish of Fátima. Francisco and Jacinta died in the influenza-pandemic years (1919, 1920); Lúcia became a religious sister and died in Coimbra on 13 February 2005.[2]
Church approval status
Approved for belief and devotion at diocesan level: after a canonical process opened on 3 May 1922, the Bishop of Leiria, José Alves Correia da Silva, in the pastoral letter 'A Divina Providência' of 13 October 1930, declared the visions of the children at the Cova da Iria 'worthy of belief' (dignas de crédito) and officially permitted the cult of Our Lady of Fátima. In Catholic doctrine such approval concerns a private revelation: it permits devotion and judges the reports credible; it does not oblige belief and is not a dogmatic definition — a distinction restated in the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith's 2000 theological commentary.[3] [8] [4]
Contemporary record
Unusually for a Marian apparition case, the final reported event of 13 October 1917 was covered by the secular Lisbon press: Avelino de Almeida's report in O Século (15 October 1917) and the photographs of the crowd published in Ilustração Portuguesa (29 October 1917) are contemporary documents, whatever one concludes about the event itself.[3] [7]
Sanctuary
The Santuário de Fátima at the Cova da Iria grew from a small chapel built by devotees in 1919 to the Basilica of Our Lady of the Rosary (dedicated 1953) and the Basilica of the Most Holy Trinity (dedicated 2007, 8,633 seats), and is today one of the most visited Marian shrines in the world; its documentation service publishes the multi-volume critical edition of the sources, the 'Documentação Crítica de Fátima'.[18] [19] [3] [9]
Reported cures
Cures have been reported at Fátima since the 1920s. The canonical commission's report of 13 April 1930 lists seventeen cases, each with a named physician's certificate and the numbered issue of 'Voz da Fátima' that carried the account, and describes sick pilgrims passing through a 'posto das verificações médicas' at the shrine. No act declaring any of those seventeen cures miraculous has been located, and no standing medical bureau comparable to the one Lourdes established in 1883 has been located either — a record of what was and was not found in the sources cited here, not a claim that none exists. The only Church acts on reported cures connected with Fátima that this chronology has located are the two decrees in the seers' cause, 28 June 1999 and 23 March 2017, which judge cures attributed to the intercession of Francisco and Jacinta Marto rather than the apparitions. This site records the reporting and the acts; it asserts no cure.[3] [20] [21]

1881 1881-09-30 Leiria, Portugal Diocese of Leiria suppressed — The see erected in 1545 is suppressed on 30 September 1881 in the liberal monarchy's reorganisation of the Portuguese dioceses, and its territory — including the parish of Fátima — passes to the Patriarchate of Lisbon. It is still suppressed in 1917, which is why the reported apparitions fall under no local bishop. 1910 1910-10-05 Lisbon, Portugal The Republic proclaimed in Portugal — The monarchy falls and the Republic is proclaimed at Lisbon on 5 October 1910. The Provisional Government that takes office is anticlerical in programme, and the Diocese of Leiria-Fátima's own historical sketch describes the years that follow as ones of confiscation and persecution — the diocese's characterisation, recorded here as its own. This is the political setting in which the 1917 reports were received and opposed; the site records it as setting, not as cause.[13] [14]
1911 1911-04-20 Lisbon, Portugal The Law of Separation of Church and State — The Provisional Government decrees the Lei da Separação do Estado das Igrejas on 20 April 1911, drafted by Afonso Costa as Minister of Justice and Worship and published in the Diário do Governo the next day. It ends the Catholic religion's standing as the religion of the State, withdraws public support from worship, places Church property under state control, removes religious teaching from state schools and subjects public acts of worship to civil authorisation. It is the legal regime under which the administrator of the county of Vila Nova de Ourém acted against the children in August 1917.[13] [14]

Loca do Cabeço and Aljustrel, Fátima, Portugal Three apparitions of an Angel reported (retrospective attestation) — According to Lúcia's later memoirs, an Angel — 'the Angel of Peace', who also called himself 'the Angel of Portugal' — appeared three times to the three children: in spring at the Loca do Cabeço, in summer at the well in the yard of Lúcia's family home, and in autumn again at the Loca do Cabeço, where he gave them Communion. The Sanctuary's own narrative dates these only by season. Note: the Angel apparitions are attested retrospectively, principally in Lúcia's Second and Fourth Memoirs (1937, 1941), not in the 1917 interrogations.[1] [2] [4]
1917 1917-05-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal First reported apparition — While pasturing sheep at the Cova da Iria, the three children reported seeing 'a Lady dressed in white, more brilliant than the sun', who said she was 'from Heaven' and asked them to return on the 13th of each month for six months.[1] [3]
1917 1917-06-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Second reported apparition — Before a group of about fifty people, the children reported a second apparition; in their account the Lady announced she would soon take Francisco and Jacinta to Heaven and asked for devotion to the Immaculate Heart of Mary.[1]
1917 1917-07-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Third reported apparition; the 'Secret' — Before a crowd estimated at 4,000–5,000, the children reported a vision they later described as including hell, and a promise of a miracle in October 'so that all may believe'. The three-part 'Secret' associated with this date is attested retrospectively: its first two parts were written down in Lúcia's Third Memoir (31 August 1941), the third part on 3 January 1944 — a sourcing caveat, not a verdict.[1] [4]
1917 1917-08-19 Valinhos, Fátima, Portugal August: children detained; apparition reported at Valinhos — On 13 August the administrator of the county of Vila Nova de Ourém took the children into custody, so they were absent from the Cova da Iria, where some 15,000–18,000 people had gathered. The children reported the month's apparition on 19 August at Valinhos, near Aljustrel.[1] [8]
1917 1917-09-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Fifth reported apparition — Before a crowd estimated at 20,000–30,000, the children reported that the Lady announced that in October Our Lord, Our Lady of Sorrows and Our Lady of Mount Carmel, and Saint Joseph with the Child Jesus would appear, and that a miracle would take place.[1]
1917 1917-10-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Sixth reported apparition; the 'Miracle of the Sun' — Before a crowd the contemporary press estimated at 50,000–70,000, the children reported that the Lady named herself 'the Lady of the Rosary' and asked for a chapel. Numerous onlookers — believers and non-believers — described unusual solar phenomena: in the words of O Século's correspondent Avelino de Almeida, the sun 'danced' ('bailou', 'segundo a típica expressão dos camponeses'). The site records what the witnesses and the press reported; it does not adjudicate the event.[1] [3] [7]
1917 1917-10-15 Lisbon, Portugal O Século publishes Avelino de Almeida's report — The anticlerical Lisbon daily O Século publishes 'Coisas espantosas! Como o sol bailou ao meio-dia em Fátima' by its special correspondent Avelino de Almeida, an eyewitness account of the crowd and the solar phenomena; on 29 October the same paper's magazine, Ilustração Portuguesa, publishes Judah Ruah's photographs of the crowd watching the sun. These press pieces are the canonical contemporary attestation of the event — reproduced in the Sanctuary's critical documentation.[3] [7]
1918 1918-01-17 Leiria, Portugal Diocese of Leiria restored by the bull 'Quo vehementius' — Benedict XV restores the Diocese of Leiria by the bull 'Quo vehementius' of 17 January 1918, as a suffragan see of the Patriarchate of Lisbon with fifty parishes; Cardinal António Mendes Belo executed it at Lisbon on 4 May 1918. The decision had been taken in Rome before the reported apparitions: Cardinal Pietro Gasparri communicated it to the nuncio on 30 April 1917, thirteen days before 13 May. The restored diocese then went two years without a resident bishop, which is why no diocesan judgment on the 1917 reports was canonically possible before 1922.[15] [16] [14]The restoration is dated here by the bull (17 January 1918). Two other dates are in the sources and neither is discarded: 30 April 1917, when Gasparri communicated that the decision had been made, and 4 May 1918, when the bull was executed at Lisbon.
1919 1919-04-04 Aljustrel, Fátima, Portugal Death of Francisco Marto — Francisco Marto dies at home in Aljustrel, aged 10, in the wake of the influenza pandemic.[2] [5]
1919 1919-04-28 – 1919-06-15 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal The Capelinha das Aparições built at the Cova da Iria — A small chapel is built at the Cova da Iria between 28 April and 15 June 1919, on the initiative of local devotees and years before the diocese ruled on the reports. An image of Our Lady of the Rosary, offered by a devotee, was blessed on 13 May 1920 and brought to the chapel about a month later; the first Mass was celebrated there on 13 October 1921.[18]

1920 1920-02-20 Lisbon, Portugal Death of Jacinta Marto — Jacinta Marto dies in Lisbon, aged 9, after a long illness following the influenza pandemic.[2] [5]
1920 1920-05-15 Leiria, Portugal First resident bishop of the restored diocese appointed — By the bull 'Commissum humilitati nostrae' of 15 May 1920, José Alves Correia da Silva is appointed to the restored see of Leiria; he is consecrated at Porto Cathedral on 25 July 1920, takes canonical possession by proxy on 4 August and makes his solemn entry into Leiria on 5 August. Until then the parish priest of Fátima had reported to the Patriarchate of Lisbon and no bishop resided in the diocese. This is the bishop who ordered the canonical process in 1922 and issued the judgment of 1930.[17] [10]
1922 1922-03-06 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Bomb attack destroys the Capelinha — In the early hours of 6 March 1922 explosive charges set in the walls of the chapel at the Cova da Iria bring down its roof; a fifth charge, placed at the root of the holm oak, did not go off. The attack is attested by letters of 6 and 9 March 1922 published in the Sanctuary's critical edition (docs. 66–67). Canon Formigão's 1923 account names individuals from Lisbon, Santarém and Vila Nova de Ourém as suspected of it — his accusation, recorded here as his, and not a judicial finding. The chapel was rebuilt and reopened on 13 January 1923.[3] [18]
1922 1922-05-03 Leiria, Portugal Canonical process opened — By a provision of 3 May 1922, José Alves Correia da Silva, bishop of the restored Diocese of Leiria, orders the canonical process on the events of Fátima and names the commission of inquiry (which included Canon Manuel Nunes Formigão, who had interrogated the children in 1917). The commission delivered its report to the bishop in 1930.[8] [3]
1922 1922-10-13 Leiria, Portugal 'Voz da Fátima' begins monthly publication — The first monthly issue of 'Voz da Fátima' appears at Leiria on 13 October 1922 with the bishop's approval, opening with the text of his provision of 3 May 1922 that named the commission of inquiry. The bulletin becomes the place where cures attributed to Our Lady of Fátima are reported: the canonical commission's 1930 report cites it, numbered issue by numbered issue, for every case it lists.[3]Two starting dates are in the sources and both are kept. A single issue titled 'A Voz de Fátima' was printed on 13 June 1922 (Documentação Crítica de Fátima, doc. 65) and was not continued; monthly publication as 'Voz da Fátima' begins on 13 October 1922 (doc. 120, fl. 45v). The event is dated by the second.
1930s
1930 1930-04-13 Leiria, Portugal Canonical commission's report: seventeen cures reported, not verified here — Canon Manuel Nunes Formigão presents to the commission named in 1922 the report it had commissioned; it is read and discussed on 13 and 14 April 1930, approved unanimously except for its chapter on the European war, and handed to the bishop (Documentação Crítica de Fátima, docs. 120–121). Among the material the report weighs is a chapter 'As curas extraordinárias' listing seventeen cases, each naming the person, the ailment, the physician who signed a certificate, and the numbered issue of 'Voz da Fátima' between 13 May 1924 and 13 December 1929 in which the account was published. What is dated and recorded here is the reporting: that these cures were claimed, attested by named doctors and published on those dates. The report's own step from them to the conclusion that 'the miracles of Fátima constitute by themselves an evident proof of the supernaturality of the apparitions' is Formigão's argument, quoted as his; no diocesan or Roman act has ever declared any of the seventeen miraculous.[3]
1930 1930-10-13 Leiria, Portugal Pastoral letter 'A Divina Providência': visions declared worthy of belief — In the pastoral letter 'A Divina Providência', Bishop José Alves Correia da Silva declares it right 'to declare worthy of belief the visions of the children at the Cova da Iria … on the 13th of May to October 1917' and 'officially to permit the cult of Our Lady of Fátima' (Documentação Crítica de Fátima, doc. 133). This is the key diocesan approval document: a judgment of credibility permitting devotion, not a dogmatic definition.[3] [8]
1935 1935–1944 Pontevedra and Tuy, Spain Lúcia's memoirs written — the source of the 'Secrets' — At her superiors' and the bishop's request, Lúcia writes four memoirs (concluded 25 December 1935, 21 November 1937, 31 August 1941, 8 December 1941). Much of the now-familiar detail — the 1916 Angel apparitions, the vision of hell, the first two parts of the 'Secret' — is attested only retrospectively through them. On 3 January 1944, by order of the Bishop of Leiria, she writes down the third part of the 'Secret', sealed and later kept by the Holy Office. The retrospective character of these texts is recorded here as a sourcing caveat, not a verdict on their content.[2] [4]

1942 1942-10-31 Vatican City Pius XII consecrates the Church and the world to the Immaculate Heart of Mary — In a radio message in Portuguese to the faithful of Portugal, sent for the jubilee solemnities in honour of the Virgin venerated at Fátima, Pius XII entrusts and consecrates to the Immaculate Heart of Mary 'not only the Holy Church … but also the whole world torn by savage discord' (Acta Apostolicae Sedis 34, 1942, pp. 313 ff.). It is an act of devotion made on a Fátima occasion; it contains no judgment on whether the 1917 apparitions were supernatural.[23]
1946 1946-05-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal The image at the Capelinha crowned by a papal legate — Cardinal Benedetto Aloisi Masella, sent as Pius XII's legate by the letter 'Celeberrima sollemnia' of 28 April 1946 (Acta Apostolicae Sedis 38, p. 376), solemnly crowns the image of Our Lady of the Rosary in the Capelinha; the crown had been offered by Portuguese women on 13 October 1942. The act honours an image. It remains the strongest candidate for a Roman act on Fátima and still decides nothing about the visions.[11] [18]
1950s
1953 1953-10-07 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Dedication of the Basilica of Our Lady of the Rosary — The basilica whose first stone had been blessed on 13 May 1928 is dedicated on 7 October 1953; Pius XII granted it the title of basilica by the brief 'Luce Superna' of 11 November 1954. The remains of the two younger seers were translated into it beforehand — Jacinta's on 1 May 1951, Francisco's in 1952.[19] [2] [20]The translation of Francisco Marto's remains is dated 13 March 1952 in the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints' file on the cause and 17 February 1952 in the Sanctuary's 'Cronologia dos videntes'. Both are recorded; neither is preferred.

1967 1967-05-13 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Paul VI's pilgrimage to Fátima — Paul VI comes to the Cova da Iria on 13 May 1967 — the fiftieth anniversary of the first reported apparition and the twenty-fifth of the 1942 consecration — and celebrates Mass in the basilica. In the homily he calls himself a 'humble and trusting pilgrim' and prays for the peace of the Church and of the world. It is the first visit by a reigning pope. It is a visit, and it rules on nothing.[24]
1990s
1999 1999-06-28 Vatican City Decree on a reported cure promulgated in the seers' cause — The Congregation for the Causes of Saints promulgates the decree on the alleged miracle in the cause of Francisco and Jacinta Marto — the act that opened the way to the beatification of 13 May 2000. The case examined was a reported cure of a Portuguese woman of 'paraplegia from probable transverse myelitis, of about twenty-two years' duration, in the absence of psychiatric pathology'; the inquiry into it was held at Leiria in 1995 and declared valid on 21 November 1997, and John Paul II's apostolic letter of 13 May 2000 places the cure itself in 1987. This event is the Church act and its date. Whether a cure occurred, and what caused it, is what the act judges — not what this dataset records.[20]

2000 2000-05-13 Fátima, Portugal Beatification of Francisco and Jacinta Marto — John Paul II beatifies Francisco and Jacinta Marto at the Sanctuary of Fátima. At the end of the Mass, Cardinal Angelo Sodano announces that the third part of the 'Secret' will be published.[5] [4]
2000 2000-06-26 Vatican City The 'Third Secret' published — The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith publishes 'The Message of Fatima': the photostatic text of the third part of the 'Secret' (written 3 January 1944), Sister Lúcia's letters, Cardinal Sodano's 13 May announcement, and a theological commentary by Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger, which reads the vision as symbolic of the Church's twentieth-century persecutions and restates that a private revelation, even an approved one, does not oblige belief.[4]
2005 2005-02-13 Coimbra, Portugal Death of Sister Lúcia, the last of the seers — Lúcia de Jesus dos Santos dies at the Carmel of Saint Teresa in Coimbra, aged 97. She had left Fátima on 15 June 1921 for the Asilo de Vilar at Porto, begun her novitiate with the Dorothean Sisters at Tuy on 2 October 1926, made perpetual vows on 3 October 1934, entered the Carmel of Coimbra on 25 March 1948 and made solemn profession there on 31 May 1949. Her remains were translated to the Basilica of Our Lady of the Rosary at Fátima on 19 February 2006, and the process for her beatification was opened on 30 April 2008.[2]
2007 2007-10-12 Cova da Iria, Fátima, Portugal Dedication of the Basilica of the Most Holy Trinity — A second and far larger church at the Cova da Iria, seating 8,633, is dedicated on 12 October 2007 by Cardinal Tarcisio Bertone as Benedict XVI's legate for the close of the ninetieth anniversary; the first stone had been laid on 6 June 2004. The Sanctuary dates the intention to build it to 1973, when the Rosary basilica was found no longer able to hold the pilgrims of an ordinary Sunday — the clearest dated measure in this chronology of how far the pilgrimage grew. The Congregation for Divine Worship granted the new church the title of basilica by decree of 19 June 2012.[19]
2010s
2017 2017-03-23 Vatican City Decree on a second reported cure promulgated in the seers' cause — Pope Francis authorises the Congregation for the Causes of Saints to promulgate the decree on the miracle attributed to the intercession of Blessed Francisco and Jacinta Marto, and the Holy See's bulletin publishes it under the heading of 23 March 2017 — the act that opened the way to the canonization of 13 May 2017. The case is a reported recovery of a child from 'severe cranio-encephalic trauma, with risk of death or of grave consequences' at Campo Mourão, Brazil, in 2013: the diocesan tribunal sat there from 5 to 13 August 2016, the Medical Consulta found the recovery scientifically inexplicable on 2 February 2017, the theological consultors reported on 21 February and the Cardinals and Bishops judged the case in ordinary session on 21 March. The dates and the acts are what this dataset records.[21] [20]Two dates circulate for this decree: 21 March 2017, the ordinary session of Cardinals and Bishops that judged the case, and 23 March 2017, the papal audience under which the dicastery's own archive files it. Both are recorded; the event is dated by the promulgation.
2017 2017-05-13 Fátima, Portugal Canonization of Francisco and Jacinta Marto — On the centenary of the first reported apparition, Pope Francis canonizes Francisco and Jacinta Marto at the Sanctuary of Fátima.[6]

The papal consecrations

Nine papal acts, from 1942 to 2022, are commonly counted as steps in "the consecration" the seers reported being asked for. They are not interchangeable. Some consecrate, some entrust, one only exhorts the faithful to consecrate themselves; they consecrate different objects; and only one names Russia while only two are made in union with the bishops of the world. This section sets each act against those two conditions, taking the answer from the act's own text.

None of this is Fátima's approval, and the dates say so before any argument does. Fátima was declared worthy of belief by the Bishop of Leiria on 13 October 1930 — twelve years before the first act below. A consecration is an act of devotion by a pope; it presupposes the devotion rather than adjudicating the apparition, which is why no act here appears as a rung on the approval ladder above and why the Holy See rung remains "no ruling located". What the sequence does show is how deeply Rome engaged with the Fátima devotion without ever ruling on the 1917 events — and that is worth a section of its own precisely because the engagement is so easily mistaken for the ruling.

1942-10-31 Pius XII

Vatican City — radio message broadcast to Portugal

consecrationRussia named: not namedWith the bishops: the pope alone

What was consecrated: The Church and the whole human race

Broadcast in Portuguese to the Portuguese nation for the jubilee of the apparitions, and the first papal consecration in this series. What it consecrates is the Church and the human race; the text on vatican.va names neither Fátima nor Russia. The nearest it comes to the second is a petition for "the peoples separated by error or by discord" — among whom, it says, there was no house without the venerable icon in a place of honour, "today perhaps hidden away and put by for better days". Readers have taken that as Russia in all but the word ever since, and the dataset records the wording rather than the reading. Pius XII is widely reported to have repeated the act in Saint Peter's on 8 December 1942. vatican.va does carry a text under that date, but in its prayers section rather than among the 1942 speeches, and it is the short first-person prayer — “ci affidiamo e ci consacriamo” — that Acta Apostolicae Sedis had already printed at 34 (1942) 345–346, in the fascicle of 23 November, with an indulgence noted 17 November, while the volume's own index dates it 31 October. Three official datings attach to one text, and the chronological index of the pope's acts in AAS 34 lists nothing whatever for 8 December: its only December item is the allocution of the 17th. So the repetition is recorded here as reported and undocumented — no act text for that day has been found, and the two texts should not be run together, the radio message being third person and the prayer first.

Ai popoli separati per l'errore o per la discordia, e segnatamente a coloro che professano per Voi singolare devozione, e presso i quali non c'era casa ove non si tenesse in onore la vostra veneranda icone (oggi forse occultata e riposta per giorni migliori), date la pace e riconduceteli all'unico ovile di Cristo, sotto l'unico e vero Pastore.

"Sacro vergente anno", dated at Saint Peter's on the feast of Saints Cyril and Methodius, consecrates the peoples of Russia by name — the first papal act in this series to do so, ten years after the general consecration it explicitly recalls. The pope says he acted on petitions asking him for exactly this. The letter runs to seven pages in the official gazette and does not mention Fátima once: the act names Russia without naming the apparition, in the same way the 1942 act named the apparition's country without naming Russia.

…quemadmodum paucis ante annis universum hominum genus Immaculato Deiparae Virginis Cordi consecravimus, ita in praesens cunctos Russiarum populos eidem Immaculato Cordi peculiarissimo modo dedicamus ac consecramus. ("…as a few years ago we consecrated the whole human race to the Immaculate Heart of the Virgin Mother of God, so now we dedicate and consecrate all the peoples of Russia in a most special way to that same Immaculate Heart.")

1964-11-21 Paul VI

Saint Peter's Basilica — close of the third session of the Second Vatican Council

entrustmentRussia named: not namedWith the bishops: the pope alone

What was consecrated: The whole human race, commended to the Immaculate Heart

Widely reported as the act by which Paul VI renewed his predecessor's consecration, and that is not what the text does. At n. 40 he recalls that Pius XII "consecrated" the world to the Immaculate Heart, says he thought it right to commemorate that act, announces a Golden Rose for the shrine of Fátima, and adds that by this gesture "we too entrust" the protection of all humanity to Mary's heavenly care. At n. 48 he commends the whole human race to her Immaculate Heart. The verbs are "affidiamo" and "raccomandiamo", not "consacriamo"; the consecration in the passage is Pius XII's, not his own. This entry keeps the distinction because the literature that reports a renewal here also tends to date the act it renews to 1952, when the world consecration was 1942 — two slips that compound.

Con questo gesto anche Noi affidiamo alla celeste tutela di Maria la protezione dell'intera umanità… Al tuo Cuore Immacolato, o Vergine Madre di Dio, raccomandiamo tutto il genere umano.

Paul VI went to the Cova da Iria on the fiftieth anniversary of the first reported apparition — the first pope to do so — and issued "Signum Magnum" the same day. The exhortation is built on the twenty-fifth anniversary of Pius XII's 1942 consecration, and what it asks for is that the sons of the Church renew personally their own consecration to the Immaculate Heart. It is included here precisely because it is not a papal consecration: an anniversary, a pilgrimage and an exhortation are three things a chronology can easily let collapse into a fourth that did not happen.

What was consecrated: The world and all peoples, in the words of the Fátima act of 1982

A renewal of the act of 13 May 1982, prayed in that act's own words. Two things establish that from the primary side rather than by inference: the Holy See's own Portuguese version of this page is subtitled “Acto de Consagração a Nossa Senhora (Fátima, 13 de maio de 1982)”, and the prayer bodies of the two acts are identical apart from punctuation, one scriptural reference and the phrase “nella vita sociale”, the 1983 text dropping only the 1982 opening about standing in the place she chose and its closing petitions about the South Atlantic. Russia is not named: the act uses the circumlocution that makes 1984 disputed — “quegli uomini e quelle Nazioni, che di questo affidamento e di questa consacrazione hanno particolarmente bisogno”. The Synod fathers were in Rome and the text says “insieme con tutta la Chiesa”, but no convocation of the world's episcopate was issued for it, so it is scored as the pope acting alone. Two corrections come with it. EWTN's list places the act at the closing Mass of the Synod on Penance and Reconciliation; the Synod closed on 29 October, whose homily contains no Marian act, and the act belongs to the Angelus of the 16th while the Synod was still sitting. And the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith's 2000 narrative passes from 1982 to 1984 without this act — a selection by that narrative, not an absence, since the text is on vatican.va. Its absence from Acta Apostolicae Sedis is likewise not evidence against it: that volume prints no Angelus addresses at all.

In modo speciale ti affidiamo e consacriamo quegli uomini e quelle Nazioni, che di questo affidamento e di questa consacrazione hanno particolarmente bisogno. […] Affidandoti, o Madre, il mondo, tutti gli uomini e tutti i popoli, ti affidiamo anche la stessa consacrazione per il mondo, mettendola nel tuo Cuore materno. Oh Cuore Immacolato!

1984-03-25 John Paul II

Saint Peter's Square, Rome — before the image brought from the Capelinha at Fátima

consecrationRussia named: not namedWith the bishops: in union with the bishops

What was consecrated: All men and women and all peoples, entrusted to the Immaculate Heart

The act the argument is about. On 8 December 1983 the pope wrote to the bishops of the world asking them to join him; on 25 March 1984, in spiritual union with those bishops, "convoked" beforehand, he entrusted all men and women and all peoples to the Immaculate Heart of Mary. Russia is not named in the text. The collegial condition is satisfied and the naming condition is not, which is exactly why the act is disputed — and why the dispute is settled, for the Holy See, by testimony rather than by the text: Sister Lúcia wrote on 8 November 1989 that "yes it has been done just as Our Lady asked, on 25 March 1984", and the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, publishing that letter in 2000, added that "hence any further discussion or request is without basis". This page records the act, the letter and the Congregation's conclusion, and does not adjudicate between them and those who keep asking.

…we entrust and consecrate to you this very consecration of the world, placing it in your motherly Heart.

Key figures

Lúcia dos Santos (Irmã Lúcia)

1907–2005 · Portugal

The eldest of the three seers and the only one to survive to adulthood; as a Dorothean sister and later a Carmelite in Coimbra she wrote the memoirs (1935–1941) and the 1944 manuscript that are the principal — and retrospective — source for the Angel apparitions and the 'Secret'.[2] [4]

Francisco Marto

1908–1919 · Portugal

Seer, brother of Jacinta and cousin of Lúcia; in the children's account he saw but did not hear the Lady. Died in 1919; beatified in 2000 and canonized in 2017.[2] [5] [6]

Jacinta Marto

1910–1920 · Portugal

The youngest seer, sister of Francisco. Died in Lisbon in 1920 after a long illness; beatified in 2000 and canonized in 2017.[2] [5] [6]

José Alves Correia da Silva

1872–1957 · Portugal

Bishop of Leiria (1920–1957). Opened the canonical process on the Fátima events in 1922 and, in the pastoral letter 'A Divina Providência' of 13 October 1930, declared the visions worthy of belief and officially permitted the cult of Our Lady of Fátima.[3] [8]

Avelino de Almeida

1873–1932 · Portugal

Journalist of the Lisbon daily O Século, present at the Cova da Iria on 13 October 1917; his reports in O Século (15 October) and Ilustração Portuguesa (29 October) are the best-known contemporary press attestation of the 'Miracle of the Sun'. He recorded what the crowd saw and cried without endorsing a supernatural reading.[3] [7]

Manuel Nunes Formigão

1883–1958 · Portugal

Canon and seminary professor who interrogated the children from September 1917 and served on the canonical commission named in 1922; his interrogations are among the earliest written records of the seers' accounts.[3] [8]

Disambiguation & nuance

Fátima is contested terrain between believers, skeptics and historians. This dataset records reports and rulings, with their dates and documents — it does not assert or deny the supernatural claim.

Approved for pilgrimage is not the same as approved as an apparition — and that is the Church's rule, not this page's

A shrine, a crowned image, a feast, indulgences, a papal visit: it is reasonable to read all that as the Church saying the apparition happened. It is not what the Church says it is doing, and since 2024 it says so in writing. The Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith's "Norms for Proceeding in the Discernment of Alleged Supernatural Phenomena" (17 May 2024) sets out six possible conclusions, and art. 23 makes the position explicit: "as a rule, neither the Diocesan Bishop, nor the Episcopal Conferences, nor the Dicastery will declare that these phenomena are of supernatural origin", though the Pope may authorise a special procedure. The ordinary favourable outcome is now "Nihil obstat", which art. 17 defines as acknowledging "many signs of the action of the Holy Spirit" in a spiritual experience "without expressing any certainty about the supernatural authenticity of the phenomenon itself". The document's own presentation adds that most shrines "have never had an official declaration of the supernatural nature of the events". The reason is what a private revelation is. Public revelation closed with the apostles, so no apparition adds an article of faith, and even an approved one obliges no one to believe — the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith said exactly that when it published the third part of the Fátima secret in 2000. What a bishop is actually asked is a pastoral question: is this devotion safe, is it free of doctrinal error, is it bearing good fruit? That can be answered yes without answering the historical question, and usually is. The same acts are granted to devotions with no apparition behind them at all — Our Lady of the Rosary and Our Lady of Mount Carmel have feasts, crowned images and basilicas, and there is no reported apparition to approve. So this page is not withholding approval; it is saying who gave which one, about what. Where a bishop did rule on an apparition, the ladder says so in his own words and marks the rung favourable — Lourdes 1862 and La Salette 1851 are quoted verbatim, Fátima 1930 is cited to its pastoral letter. What the ladder refuses to do is upgrade a crown into a verdict. If it did, a case a bishop examined and endorsed would look identical to a case nobody ever ruled on, because both have pilgrims; and the reverse error would follow just as easily, reading a Roman act against one seer's later writings as Rome condemning the apparition. Keeping the objects apart is the only way the chart carries information at all. Where the sources themselves say the Church's recognition is "implicit", the page records that word too, and does not promote it.[32]

Reported apparition ≠ asserted fact

Every apparition entry in this chronology is a report: the children said they saw the Lady; the press said the crowd saw the sun move. The Church's 1930 ruling judged the visions 'worthy of belief' — a credibility judgment permitting devotion. None of these records, nor this site, asserts the supernatural event as established fact; Cardinal Ratzinger's 2000 commentary itself notes that assent to an approved private revelation is not obligatory for Catholics.[3] [4]

The 1917 record ≠ the memoirs

What was written down in 1917 (parish and Formigão interrogations, press reports) is much sparser than the now-familiar narrative. The Angel apparitions of 1916, the vision of hell and the three-part 'Secret' are attested principally in Lúcia's memoirs of 1935–1941 and her 1944 manuscript — retrospective sources written 18–27 years after the events. Recording this gap is a sourcing caveat, not a verdict on their truthfulness.[4] [2] [3]

'Worthy of belief' ≠ dogma

The 1930 formula 'dignas de crédito' (worthy of belief) authorizes public devotion and states that the reports show signs of credibility. It binds no one to believe: in Catholic teaching Fátima is a private revelation, distinct from the deposit of faith — a distinction the 2000 Vatican commentary restates explicitly.[3] [4]

Contemporary press attestation ≠ press endorsement

O Século was an anticlerical republican paper; Avelino de Almeida reported what tens of thousands present said they saw ('the sun danced', as he put it, 'in the peasants' typical expression') and framed 'miracle' as the crowd's cry, not the paper's conclusion. Citing his report attests the event's contemporaneity and scale, not a supernatural explanation.[3] [7]
